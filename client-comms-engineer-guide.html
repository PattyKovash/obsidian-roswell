Client Communication
for Technical People

You're the technical expert in the room. This guide covers how to communicate that expertise clearly on calls and in emails — especially with non-technical clients.

01

Your role on a client call

Most calls are led by the Merchant Partner. Your job is to be the technical expert — not to run the meeting, but to step in when a question needs a real answer, when something is technically infeasible, or when the client needs to understand a constraint before making a decision.

Three things to keep in mind before every call:

  • You're there to inform, not to impress. Clients don't need to know how hard something was. They need to know if it's done, when it'll be done, and whether it does what they need.
  • Short answers first, detail on request. Lead with the bottom line. If they want to go deeper, they'll ask.
  • You always know more than the client about the code — and less about their business. Respect both.
If solo

If you're running a call without the MP, open with a brief framing of what you'll cover, confirm the client's priority for the session, and close by restating next steps and owners clearly. Structure prevents drift.

02

Translating for non-technical clients

Most Roswell clients are not developers. They run e-commerce businesses. They care about revenue, conversion, speed, and whether the thing works. When you speak in technical terms, you put the burden of translation on them — and they will often nod along rather than admit they don't follow. That's how misaligned expectations happen.

The rule: lead with business impact, follow with the technical detail only if they ask.

Technical framing
Client framing
"We refactored the cart to use the Storefront API."means nothing to them
"We rewrote how the cart loads — it's faster and more reliable now, especially on mobile."what they actually care about
"The ReCharge widget has a DOM conflict with the checkout extension."will cause confusion
"There's a conflict between two tools in your checkout — we're resolving it before we ship."clear and not alarming
"LCP regressed by 400ms after the script injection."
"The update slowed the page load slightly — we've already fixed it."
"We can't do that in Liquid without breaking the OS2 schema."
"Shopify doesn't support that in the theme layer — we'd need a different approach. Here's what does work."
"The metafield namespace is misconfigured."
"Some of your product data wasn't connected correctly — we fixed the link."

You don't need to dummy anything down. You need to meet clients at the level of outcomes they actually care about. If they want the technical detail, they'll ask for it — and that's when you go deep.

Useful habit

Before a call, for every technical item you might be asked about, practice finishing this sentence: "From your end, what this means is…" That's the version you lead with.

03

What to say / not say

Some phrases shut conversations down or erode client confidence. Others build it. The difference is usually specificity, tone, and whether you're centering the client's concern or your own process.

Don't say
Say instead
"That's not possible."sounds like a dead end
"Shopify doesn't support that directly — here's the closest thing that works and what the tradeoff is."
"That's easy / that's simple."raises expectations, often wrong
"That's straightforward — I can have it in front of you by [date]."
"That's really complex."sounds like deflection
"That one has a few moving parts — let me think it through and give you a proper estimate."
"I'll try."plants doubt
"I'll have a clear answer for you by [time/date]."
"The previous developer did this wrong."unprofessional, unhelpful
"The code here needs some cleanup before we build on it — we'll handle it."
"I don't know."okay alone — not alone
"I want to look at that properly before I give you a confident answer — I'll follow up by [time]."
"We found a bug."alarms without context
"We caught an issue before it reached customers and it's already fixed."
"It works on my end."shifts blame
"I'm not seeing it reproduce here — can you walk me through what you're seeing? I want to make sure we're looking at the same thing."

The goal isn't to spin things. It's to communicate clearly. Clients can handle technical constraints — what erodes trust is vagueness, deflection, and surprises they weren't warned about.

04

When you don't know

Not knowing something on a call is completely normal. The mistake is how people handle it: either they guess (and are wrong), or they go silent, or they apologize excessively. None of those build confidence.

The right move is to say you'll find out, give them a specific time when you will, and follow through. That is itself a demonstration of competence.

Scripts for not knowing

When a client asks a technical question you can't answer on the spot

"Good question — I want to give you an accurate answer rather than guess. Let me check on that and get back to you [today / by end of week]. Is that timeline okay for you?"

When you're asked if something is possible and you genuinely don't know

"I don't want to commit to that without understanding your setup better — can I dig into it and come back to you with a proper answer? I'd rather do that than give you a number that turns out to be wrong."

When a question is outside your area and the MP isn't on the call

"That's a great question for [MP name] — it touches on the commercial side of the project. I'll make sure they follow up with you directly. On the technical piece, here's what I can tell you…"

Always

Close every "I don't know" with a specific follow-up commitment: who will respond, and by when. Then follow through. Reliability on small promises builds the same trust as getting the answer right.

05

Framing difficult topics

Sometimes you have to deliver news the client won't like — a limitation, a scope expansion, a delay, or a tradeoff. How you frame it determines whether it damages the relationship or strengthens it.

Delivering a constraint or platform limitation

Don't just say what can't be done. Immediately follow with what can.

Platform limitation weak

"Unfortunately, Shopify doesn't allow that in the checkout. We can't do it."

Platform limitation better

"Shopify restricts what can be modified in the checkout directly — it's a platform-wide constraint, not specific to your store. The good news is there's a supported way to get close to the same result using [post-purchase flow / checkout extension / alternative]. Want me to walk through what that would look like?"

Delivering a scope expansion

Frame it as information, not a negotiation. Give them the options clearly.

Scope expansion weak

"This is more work than we thought. It's going to take longer."

Scope expansion better

"When we got into the build, we found that [X] is connected to [Y] in a way that wasn't visible at scoping — getting this right means also addressing that. That adds roughly [estimate] to the timeline. I want to be upfront about that now rather than deliver it late. We can either include it, defer it to a future sprint, or I can show you a version that works without it."

Delivering a delay

Early is always better than late. Never wait until the deadline to flag a slip.

Delay example

"I want to flag early that [ticket] is running behind the original estimate — I found [specific issue] that added complexity. I'm expecting to have it ready by [new date] instead of [original date]. Nothing else in the sprint is affected."

Pattern

For any difficult topic, the structure is: what happened → why → what the options are. Don't arrive with just the problem. Arrive with at least one path forward.

06

Email best practices

Client emails are a written record. They set expectations, document decisions, and reflect on the agency. The standard for email is the same as for everything else: clear, specific, and action-oriented. Nothing vague, nothing buried, nothing that requires the client to read twice to understand what's being asked of them.

  • One email, one purpose. Don't bundle an update, a question, and a decision request into the same message.
  • State the ask in the first two sentences. If you need something from them, say so at the top — not after four paragraphs of context.
  • Use plain language. If you wouldn't say the word out loud on a call, don't write it in an email.
  • Specify next steps with owners and dates. "We'll be in touch" is not an action. "I'll send the updated timeline by Thursday" is.
  • Keep it short. If it's longer than 150 words, ask yourself what you can cut.

Follow-up after a call

Call follow-up — template
Subject: [Merchant name] — [brief topic, e.g. "Sprint 12 recap"] Hi [Name], Thanks for the time today. Quick recap of what we covered: What we decided: — [Decision 1, one sentence] — [Decision 2, one sentence] Next steps: — [Action] — [Owner], by [Date] — [Action] — [Owner], by [Date] Open item (if any): — [What's pending, who owns the answer, by when] Let me know if I missed anything. [Your name]

Flagging a technical issue by email

Issue flag — template
Subject: [Merchant name] — heads up on [brief description] Hi [Name], Flagging something we found during [build / testing / review]. What we found: [One sentence. What the issue is, from the customer's perspective if relevant.] Current status: [Fixed and shipped / In progress / Needs your input before we proceed.] What we need from you (if anything): [Specific ask, or "Nothing — just keeping you informed."] [Your name]
Tone check

Before sending, read it as if you're the client receiving it. Does it tell them what they need to know? Is it clear what, if anything, they need to do? If you'd have to follow up to clarify, rewrite it first.

07

Gathering technical requirements

The biggest source of rework isn't bad code — it's building the wrong thing. Clients often describe what they want in terms of a solution they've already imagined. Your job is to get underneath that to the actual problem, because the solution they're describing may not be the best one, or may not be feasible, or may reveal five other things that need to happen first.

This is a soft skill disguised as a technical one. You're not just collecting specs — you're figuring out why something matters to the business.

"A client asking for a 'buy one get one' feature might actually need to improve conversion on slow-moving inventory. Understanding that changes every decision you make about how to build it."

Start with the why, not the what

Before you dig into how something should work, understand why it needs to exist. This single habit surfaces more useful information than any technical checklist.

Jumping to the what
Starting with the why
"So the button should be in the cart drawer — what color and size?"collecting specs before understanding the goal
"Before we get into the details — what's the customer behavior you're trying to change with this?"reveals the actual goal
"Got it — we'll add a pop-up after checkout."accepting the solution at face value
"What's the outcome you're hoping the pop-up drives? That'll help me figure out the best place and way to implement it."tests whether a pop-up is actually the right answer
"We can build a custom filter for that."jumping to implementation
"Walk me through the customer journey — where are they when they need to filter, and what are they trying to find?"may reveal that a filter isn't the right solution at all

Questions that surface the real requirement

These work across almost any feature request. You don't need all of them — pick the ones most likely to reveal something the client hasn't told you yet.

Question
What it surfaces
"What problem does this solve for your customer?"
Whether the request is customer-driven or internal assumption. Often reveals the actual goal.
"What does success look like — how will you know this is working?"
Forces a measurable outcome. Prevents "can you just make it better" specs.
"Has anything like this been tried before? What happened?"
Reveals constraints, past failures, and context you'd otherwise discover mid-build.
"Who is the customer doing this — can you describe them?"
Surfaces mobile vs desktop, guest vs logged-in, B2B vs DTC — before you've written a line.
"Is there an edge case or exception you're worried about?"
Clients often know the tricky cases but don't think to mention them. This pulls them out.
"What's the consequence if this doesn't work correctly?"
Tells you how critical it is. High consequence = you need more detail before starting, not less.
"Is there anything in your store today that does something similar?"
May reveal an existing pattern to build on, or a conflict you'll run into.
"Are there any tools or apps managing this today that we need to work around?"
The single best question for avoiding app conflicts before they become production incidents.
"When does this need to be live — is there a specific date or event driving it?"
Reveals urgency you might not have known, and whether the timeline is negotiable or fixed.

Strategies for requirements conversations

  • Reflect back what you heard before moving on. "So if I'm understanding correctly, the goal is [X] — is that right?" This catches misalignment early and signals to the client that you're listening, not just collecting tasks.
  • Ask for an example of the behavior they want. "Can you walk me through what a customer would do, step by step?" Abstract requests become concrete fast when someone has to narrate the flow.
  • Separate the must-haves from the nice-to-haves explicitly. "If we could only do one thing here, what would it be?" Clients often present a wish list as a requirements list. This forces prioritization.
  • Name your assumptions out loud. "I'm going to assume this applies to logged-in customers only — is that right?" Stated assumptions get corrected. Unstated ones become bugs.
  • Don't accept "like Amazon does it" as a spec. Ask them to be specific about what part of the Amazon experience they're describing — clients often have a detail in mind that doesn't match the feature they're referencing.
  • If a request is vague, ask for the failure case. "What would a bad version of this look like?" is sometimes easier to answer than "what do you want?" and gives you the same information.
  • End every requirements conversation with a written summary. Send it before you start building. It takes 5 minutes and prevents days of rework.

Recognizing when you don't have enough

You have enough information to start when you can answer all four of these:

#
You can clearly state...
1
Why the client needs this — the business or customer problem it solves.
2
Who the end user is and what they're doing when they encounter this feature.
3
What "working correctly" looks like — at least one concrete success scenario.
4
What constraints exist — apps, platform limits, performance expectations, hard deadlines.

If you can't answer one of these, that's the gap to close before you open an AI session — not after.

Remember

A client describing a solution is not giving you requirements — they're giving you a starting point. Your job is to understand the problem well enough that if their proposed solution turns out to be wrong, you can still deliver something that works.
